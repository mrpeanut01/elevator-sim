# Elevator Sim — Playtest Report and Product Point of View

Tester: Claude (Cowork), playing the build at elevsim-app.salmonstone-4576d6f7.eastus2.azurecontainerapps.io
Scope: full pass through onboarding, Scenarios, Free play, This week's challenge, Leaderboard, Account, Settings, the Dispatcher Lab, Compare, Traffic, Building, Machines, and Parameters surfaces, plus several live shift runs and one 100-run statistical batch comparison.

## The short version

Elevator Sim is not a light arcade game wearing an elevator skin. It is a genuinely rigorous vertical-transportation simulator, built by someone who knows the domain (CIBSE traffic templates, up-peak detection, contract-net auctions, destination dispatch, confidence intervals on paired runs) and who refuses to let the product lie to the player about what a single run can and cannot prove. That honesty is the project's biggest asset and, right now, also its biggest barrier to a wide audience. The simulation core, the dispatcher lab, and the Day report are already excellent. The parts that turn this into something a stranger opens, gets hooked by, and comes back to tomorrow are thinner: the very first thing a new player can hit is a total system meltdown with jargon on screen, the leaderboards are empty, and there is little sensory reward for a good day.

The recommendations below are organized the way the user asked: what happens before a shift starts, what happens during a shift (including what you can touch on the dispatcher while it runs), and what happens between days. Specific, filable issues are listed at the end and have been logged to the repository.

## What already works and should not be disturbed

The three-mode split (Scenarios, Free play, This week's challenge) is the right shape. Scenarios teaches through a hand-built curriculum of eight buildings, each with a one-line lesson ("teaches that spare cars are not the same as a short interval"). Free play is correctly scoped as day-one-only so a leaderboard run stays replayable. The weekly challenge fixes the building and traffic and asks only "whose dispatcher is better," which is the cleanest possible framing for a skill leaderboard.

The Day report is the standout screen in the whole product. After a run it does not just show numbers, it explains causally where the day went wrong ("Floor P1 stacked 201 deep… every car was committed elsewhere when the calls landed together"), states plainly what the shift asked for versus what happened, and hands the player concrete levers ("Add a car," "Zone the tower," "Ask where they're going") each annotated with why that lever would have mattered today. Then it is honest that one run is an anecdote and points the player at Compare for anything resembling proof. Very few simulation games are this disciplined about not overclaiming, and it should be the model the rest of the interface follows.

The seed-and-replay system (every run is deep-linked with building, seed, and mode in the URL, plus Save recording, Load recording, and Verify replay) is a strong technical foundation for sharing runs and for building trust in the leaderboard later.

The dispatcher Lab curriculum, which stages a player from "one shaft, low demand" up through statistically judged goals like beat-the-baseline and no-divergence, is close to a genre of its own: a properly gamified introduction to traffic-engineering optimization. Nothing else in the build comes as close to being a real, structured "build your own dispatcher and prove it" game loop as this does.

## Pre-game: choosing what you are about to run

The user's instinct, pick a building and set it up before you play, is already implemented, but the path is inconsistent across modes. In Scenarios the building is fixed and the interesting pre-game choice is the Commission the building screen: a genuinely good capital-budget mechanic where more shafts, faster cars, or a taller-rated machine class each cost against a fixed "capital unit" ceiling, and the choice is locked in before the week starts. In Free play the building, dispatcher, traffic shape, arrival rate, and shift length are six independent dropdowns with no preview of expected difficulty and no guardrail. Free play's defaults on a fresh visit are Midtown Office (1,710 people, the second-hardest scenario building) paired with Conventional collective (the plainest baseline dispatcher). Pressing Start with no changes reliably saturates the building: worst wait over 1,100 seconds, only 20 percent of riders away inside a minute. That is the very first hands-on experience many players will have if they skip Scenarios and go straight to Free play, and it teaches nothing except that the game is broken, because nothing on screen at that point tells a new player this building was expected to be hard.

Recommendation: Free play should default to an easy, winnable pairing (something in the Garden Apartments range) and should show a lightweight difficulty signal, even just "population per car," before the player commits. The building-picker dropdown already prints floor and population counts; extending that to a plain-language load estimate would cost little and prevent a bad first impression.

## During the shift: what you can actually touch, and how it reads

This is where the split between Casual and Engineer detail levels matters most, and where it currently under-delivers on its own premise. Switching to Casual softens the scenario blurb copy, but the live simulation still prints "SATURATED — AWT suppressed" across the top of the canvas, the dispatcher-picker cards on the right still show raw cost formulas like `cost = 1.00·wait`, and the Day report retains terms like "peak-5min window" and "confidence interval." A mode named "plain language" should mean the player is never asked to know what AWT stands for. Right now Casual is a lighter engineer mode, not a different audience's mode.

What you can change mid-run is more limited than the user's framing suggested, and that is probably correct rather than a gap: the dispatcher itself, its weight sliders, and the building's traffic and physical parameters are all pre-run configuration, edited on the Dispatcher, Traffic, Building, and Machines tabs and then locked in for the shift that follows. What is genuinely live during playback is the clock (play, step-frame, five speed multipliers, loop), the seed and replay tools, and the landing/bank filters on the visualization. That is a sound design decision: dispatching in this genre is a policy you commit to and then observe, not a joystick you wiggle in real time. The interface should say this more directly, though, because right now a first-time player can plausibly expect to grab a car mid-run and gets no in-context explanation of why they cannot.

The building visualization itself is functionally clear (columns per shaft, a colored bar per car, up or down carets, queue counts per landing) but visually flat: a schematic elevation diagram with no motion easing, no door animation beyond a glyph swap, and no people. That is a defensible default for the Engineer view, where legibility beats spectacle, but Casual mode is the one place a more inviting, almost Two Point Hospital or Prison Architect style rendering, cars gliding, tiny figures boarding, a door that visibly opens, would do more for the game's popularity than any single numeric feature on this list. The simulation math does not need to change at all for this; it is purely a rendering layer on top of the same car and floor state the schematic view already reads from.

## Between days: progression, streaks, and the social loop

Scenario mode's day-to-day loop is the strongest part of the "meta-game." Clearing a day banks it, the left rail tracks a visible streak and a "best day so far" bar, and clearing a scenario's full quota rolls the player straight into the next scenario with "Take the next assignment." That transition felt good in testing: instant, no loading screen, no confirmation dialog in the way.

Two things undercut it. First, the Day report's "what moved since the run before this one" comparison is a nice idea, showing the delta in carried, average wait, and so on against your last run, but it compares across completely different contexts. After finishing a Free play run on Midtown Office, the next comparison shown was against a Scenario run on a different building entirely, because the game only remembers "your last run," not "your last run on this track." A diff table that says "carried was 726, now 48" reads as a wild swing rather than the two unrelated numbers it actually is. That comparison should be scoped to the same building-and-mode context, or dropped when the previous run does not share one.

Second, and more importantly for the "wildly popular" goal, the social layer that is supposed to reward mastery is currently empty. This week's challenge is a well-designed format (same seeds, dispatcher is the only variable, five-seed sets scored as a whole rather than piecemeal), but the Leaderboard for it currently shows zero posted scores, with only a labeled mock row explaining what a populated board would look like. A leaderboard with nobody on it does not create competitive pull; it creates the impression the game has no players. Before any marketing push, this needs either seed data (the project's own baseline dispatchers, run and posted as reference rows) or a reframed empty state that invites "be the first" rather than showing a placeholder that looks unfinished.

## Prioritized, filable recommendations

The following have been logged as individual issues on the repository, ranked roughly by expected impact on first-time retention and word-of-mouth potential:

1. Free play defaults to a building and dispatcher pairing that saturates on first run, with no difficulty preview beforehand.
2. Casual — plain language mode leaves engineering jargon (SATURATED, AWT, cost formulas, confidence interval language) visible across the simulation and dispatcher panels.
3. Leaderboard and This week's challenge currently show no real or seeded scores, undermining the competitive hook for new visitors.
4. The Day report's run-to-run comparison mixes unrelated buildings and modes, producing misleading deltas.
5. The live building visualization has no motion, door animation, or human figures, even in Casual mode, which limits the game's visual appeal to newcomers.
6. Nothing in the simulation screen explains, in the moment, why the dispatcher and building controls are locked once a shift is running, which can read as missing functionality rather than a deliberate design choice.
7. The "completed" label beneath the canvas can appear while the shift is still mid-playback (it reflects arrival-generation finishing, not the shift ending), which is easy to misread as the run being over.

## Closing note on identity

The single biggest strategic question for this project is not a bug, it is a positioning choice: is this primarily a teaching tool and research sandbox for people who already care about vertical transportation, or a mass-market management-sim game that happens to be built on real traffic engineering? The current build is superb at the former and has the bones for the latter. The recommendations above are aimed at making the on-ramp to the second audience much shorter without watering down the rigor that makes the first audience love it, since nothing here requires removing the Engineer mode, the Parameters schema, or the statistical Compare tool. It only asks that Casual mode, Free play's defaults, and the leaderboards actually behave like the front door for people who have never heard the word "dispatcher" before.
